How it works

Universe. 22 asset classes in six sleeves, chosen because they respond differently to growth, inflation, stress, interest rates and the dollar: Treasuries at three maturities, TIPS, investment-grade and high-yield credit, US, European and emerging-market equities, seven US sectors, real estate, gold, silver, broad commodities and Bitcoin. Money not allocated sits in T-bills. Gauges and the backtest use US-listed ETFs with long histories; the table above lists the UCITS ETF (or ETP for Bitcoin, which cannot be UCITS) a European investor would buy for each.

Gauges. Four inputs, each scored −1 to +1, are averaged into an absolute score:

  • Trend (25%): price against its 200-day average and 12-month momentum (skipping the last month), both scaled by the asset's volatility. Time-series momentum works across asset classes (Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen, 2012).
  • Relative strength (25%): rank among all investable assets by risk-adjusted 6- and 12-month momentum (cross-sectional momentum).
  • Value (20%): for equities, sectors, real assets and Bitcoin, the 4-year return ending a year ago, ranked and reversed: long-term losers tend to recover ("value and momentum everywhere", Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, 2013). TIPS use the 10-year real yield and corporates their credit spread, against the last 10 years. Treasuries get their value from the Treasury Timing gauges.
  • Macro (30%): the asset's sensitivities times six macro factors (growth, inflation, stress, falling rates, dollar, falling real yields). The three Treasury gauges use the full Treasury Timing models instead.

Rotation is a relative decision: money has to go somewhere. So the published gauge is 50% the absolute score and 50% the asset's rank among all assets on that score. The best asset sits at +50 or more and the worst at −50 or less whenever their absolute scores are on the right side of zero, which is why every zone is occupied at every rebalance. The blend changed the backtest Sharpe by less than 0.02 at every level tried (0%, 30%, 50%, 70%), so it was not chosen for returns.

Allocation. Start from a static all-weather mix: about 30% equities (half US, the rest Europe, emerging markets and sectors), 50% bonds, 17% real assets and 3% Bitcoin. Multiply each asset's base weight by its gauge: −50 or lower gives 0×, 0 gives 1×, +50 gives 2×, up to 2.5×. No asset may exceed 25% (Bitcoin 6%). If the total exceeds 100% everything is scaled down; anything below 100% goes to T-bills. Rebalancing happens on the first trading day of each month; orders fill at the next day's open.

Backtest. Run in tradelab, with weights decided at the close and filled at the next open, and 10 bp of costs per trade (5 commission + 5 slippage). It passes tradelab's causality checks (prefix invariance, future poison) on every run, and a separate test in this repo re-runs the model on data truncated at three dates (macro included) to confirm nothing uses the future. The tilt strength (1×) and monthly rebalancing were fixed before the grid was run.

Caveats. Bitcoin's 2017 run lifts both all-weather versions; the "without Bitcoin" switch shows the gauges' edge without it. Results are in US dollars: a euro investor also carries currency risk unless they use hedged share classes. UCITS ETFs track the same indices as the US proxies but differ in fees, tax treatment and history. The engine holds constant weights between rebalances, so drift is not modelled. The macro sensitivities were set by judgement, not estimated. tradelab's overall verdict is red because of PBO (see above). Nothing here is investment advice.
