Learn · Lecture 1

How dollars are made and how the Fed moves them

A full lecture on the plumbing behind every asset price: who creates money, what the Federal Reserve actually does when it "prints", QE versus QT, repo and reverse repo, and how the Fed stepped in when markets broke in March 2020. Every chart uses the Fed's own published data, updated weekly.

New to this? Read this first. You don't need any background in economics. Chapter 1 builds the five ideas the rest of the lecture uses: balance sheets, interest rates, bonds, the main players, and what the Fed is for. Every later chapter opens with a one-paragraph summary, ends with a short recap, and has a couple of quick questions to check yourself. If a word is unfamiliar, it's in the glossary.

The whole story in six sentences

  1. Most of the money people use is bank deposits, and banks create them when they make loans.
  2. Banks pay each other with a different kind of money, reserves, which only the Fed can create.
  3. The Fed's main job is to steer interest rates so that inflation stays near 2% and as many people as possible have jobs.
  4. It does that by choosing what interest it pays and charges on reserves, which sets the floor and ceiling for the rate banks charge each other overnight.
  5. When cutting rates to zero is not enough, it buys bonds in bulk with new reserves (QE), and later lets them run off (QT).
  6. In a panic it lends to almost anyone with good collateral, which is why its balance sheet jumps in every crisis.

Chapter 1

Start here: five ideas you need

Money mechanics look confusing mostly because people skip the basics. These five ideas take ten minutes and make every later chapter much easier. Don't rush them.

Idea 1: a balance sheet is a list of what you own and what you owe

Every person, company and bank, including the Fed, can be described by a balance sheet, a two-column list:

  • Assets (left column): things you own or are owed. Your house, your car, the money in your bank account.
  • Liabilities (right column): things you owe to someone else. Your mortgage, your credit card balance.
  • Equity (also on the right): what is left over if you sold every asset and paid every debt. For a person it's called net worth; for a bank, its capital.

Because equity is defined as "assets minus liabilities", the two columns always add up to the same total. That's why it's called a balance sheet, and it's why every transaction in this lecture changes at least two lines at once.

The single most useful trick: one person's asset is another person's liability. The $20 in your checking account is an asset to you and a debt your bank owes you. Your mortgage is a debt to you and an asset (a loan) to the bank. Look at the two balance sheets below and find the mirror images.

Idea 2: an interest rate is the price of borrowing money

If you borrow $100 for a year at 5%, you pay back $105. The extra $5 is rent for using someone else's money. Interest rates are quoted per year, even for loans that last one night: a 4% overnight loan of $1 million costs about $111 (4% ÷ 360 days × $1M).

Rates differ by how long you borrow (a 30-year loan usually costs more than a 1-night loan, because a lot can go wrong in 30 years) and by how risky the borrower is (the US government pays less than a start-up). The Fed directly controls only the shortest rate of all, the overnight rate between banks. Everything else is priced by markets on top of it.

Rate changes are measured in basis points (bp): one basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. Going from 4.00% to 4.25% is "up 25 bp". The Fed almost always moves in steps of 25 bp.

Idea 3: a bond is a tradeable IOU, and its price moves opposite to interest rates

A bond is a loan cut into pieces that investors can buy and sell. A US Treasury bond is a loan to the US government: you pay $100 today, the government pays you a fixed coupon (say $3) every year, and returns your $100 when the bond matures. Short-dated ones (a year or less) are called Treasury bills.

Here is the key fact. Suppose you own that bond paying $3 a year, and new bonds now pay $5. Nobody will pay you $100 for your $3 bond when $100 buys a $5 bond, so to sell it you must cut the price until its return matches. When interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall; when rates fall, prices rise. The return a buyer locks in at today's price is the bond's yield.

Longer bonds swing more, because you're stuck with the old coupon for more years. Drag the slider and compare the three lines. This idea comes back in the QE chapter, and in the failure of Silicon Valley Bank.

Idea 5: what the Fed is for

Congress gave the Federal Reserve a dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices. The Fed defines stable prices as 2% inflation a year. Inflation is the rate at which prices in general go up: at 2%, a basket of groceries that costs $100 today costs $102 next year. Too much inflation erodes wages and savings; too little (or falling prices, called deflation) tends to come with weak economies and rising debt burdens.

The Fed's main lever is the price of money. Cheaper borrowing encourages people and companies to borrow, spend and hire; that heats up the economy and, eventually, prices. Dearer borrowing cools it down. The chain looks like this, and it takes roughly a year or more to work through:

Fed sets the overnight rate→ Bills, savings accounts, credit cards reprice within days→ Markets reprice mortgages and long bonds on what they expect next→ Borrowing, spending, hiring, stock prices and the dollar respond→ Jobs and inflation move

The decisions are made by the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC): seven governors in Washington plus five of the twelve regional Fed bank presidents, meeting eight times a year. The Fed is independent: the government appoints its leaders but doesn't approve each decision, so rates can't easily be cut just before an election.

Remember
  • Assets = liabilities + equity, always. Every transaction changes at least two lines.
  • My asset is your liability: your deposit is a debt your bank owes you.
  • Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions, and long bonds move most.
  • The Fed's goals are 2% inflation and maximum employment; its main tool is the overnight interest rate.

Chapter 2

What is a dollar?

In plain English: "a dollar" can mean three different things, a banknote, a bank's balance at the Fed, or the number in your bank account. They're all worth exactly $1, but different institutions owe them and different people can hold them.

A dollar is not one thing. It is a promise recorded on somebody's balance sheet (Idea 1), and there are three kinds of promise that all trade at exactly one dollar:

  • Physical currency. A $20 note is a liability of the Federal Reserve: it's printed on the note, "Federal Reserve Note". Anyone can hold it.
  • Bank reserves. Balances that commercial banks keep in their accounts at the Fed. Think of it as the bank's own checking account, held at the bank for banks. Only banks (and a few other institutions) can hold them, and they never leave the Fed's books. Banks use them to pay each other.
  • Bank deposits. The number in your checking account is a liability of your bank, not of the Fed. This is the money households and firms actually spend, and it is by far the largest layer.

Why is a banknote a "liability" if the Fed won't give you anything for it? Until 1971 dollars could, in principle, be swapped for gold. Today the Fed owes you nothing except another note, yet it still records currency as a liability, because the note is a claim on the Fed that the Fed must accept back (for example, when a bank returns cash). People value it because the government accepts it for taxes and the law makes it legal tender for debts. Think of it like casino chips: the casino records the chips you hold as something it owes, and everyone in the building treats them as money.

Why there are two kinds of "bank" money

The US runs a two-tier system. The public pays each other with bank deposits. Banks pay each other with reserves. Here's what happens when you pay a friend who banks somewhere else:

  1. You send Alice $100. Your bank (Bank A) lowers your deposit by $100.
  2. Alice's bank (Bank B) raises her deposit by $100. Bank B now owes Alice $100 more, so it needs to be paid.
  3. Bank A pays Bank B by moving $100 of reserves from its account at the Fed to Bank B's account at the Fed.

The total amount of reserves didn't change: they just moved from one bank to another, inside the Fed. That's an important rule: banks can't create or destroy reserves, and the public can't hold them. Only the Fed changes the total. When you hear "banks are sitting on trillions of reserves instead of lending them out", that's a misunderstanding: reserves can't be lent to households at all. They can only move between banks, or turn into cash when someone withdraws notes.

The layers form a hierarchy. Deposits are promises to pay you reserves-or-cash on demand; reserves and cash are promises of the central bank, which can never run out of its own liabilities.

Chapter 3

How dollars are made: the money lab

In plain English: a bank doesn't lend out somebody else's savings. It writes a new number into your account and calls it a loan. That's how most money is born. The Fed creates reserves and cash, by buying things and paying with money it creates.

The textbook story says banks collect savings and lend them out. The real mechanics run the other way: when a bank makes a loan, it creates a brand-new deposit for the borrower. Loans create deposits; repaying a loan destroys them. The Bank of England put this plainly in its 2014 paper Money creation in the modern economy.

A loan, step by step

Sam borrows $30,000 to buy a car. The bank checks Sam's income, signs the contract, and then simply types $30,000 into Sam's account. On the bank's balance sheet two lines grow at once: a new asset (Sam owes the bank $30,000) and a new liability (the bank owes Sam a $30,000 deposit). No other customer's balance went down. $30,000 of money exists that didn't exist a minute ago.

Sam pays the car dealer, so the deposit moves to the dealer's bank, along with $30,000 of reserves (as in the payment example in Chapter 2). The money still exists, now in the dealer's account. It only disappears when Sam repays the loan: his deposit shrinks and the loan shrinks with it.

So what stops banks creating unlimited money?

  • Profitability. Every loan must earn more than it costs the bank to fund it, after expected losses. Bad loans lose money.
  • Capital requirements. Regulators make banks fund part of every loan with their own equity (Idea 1), roughly 10% or more for risky loans. Equity is expensive to raise, so it rations lending.
  • Liquidity. When borrowers spend their new money, it flows to other banks and the lender must hand over reserves. A bank has to keep enough reserves, or be able to borrow them, to cover those outflows.
  • Demand. Someone must want to borrow, at a rate the bank will accept. Higher Fed rates make loans dearer and reduce demand.

Old textbooks taught a "money multiplier": banks must hold a fixed fraction of deposits as reserves, so the Fed controls deposits by controlling reserves. It never worked well in practice, and since March 2020 US reserve requirements are zero, so it describes nothing at all.

Where the Fed and Treasury fit

The Fed creates the other two layers. It creates reserves whenever it buys an asset or lends, simply by crediting a bank's account. It issues currency when banks order cash: the Bureau of Engraving and Printing prints the notes, and a bank pays for them with reserves. The Treasury does not create money; it taxes and borrows, and keeps its cash in an account at the Fed called the Treasury General Account (TGA). That last detail matters: when money moves into the TGA, it leaves the banking system, and when the Treasury spends, it comes back.

Chapter 4

How the Fed "buys money"

In plain English: the Fed buys bonds and pays by typing new reserves into the seller's bank account. Nothing is printed. Whether this adds to the money people hold depends on who sold the bond.

The phrase "the Fed prints money" is shorthand for something more precise. The Fed's trading desk at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York buys Treasury bonds or mortgage-backed securities from about two dozen primary dealers. It pays by crediting the dealer's bank with reserves that did not exist a second earlier. No paper is printed. The bonds go into the Fed's portfolio, the System Open Market Account (SOMA).

What does it buy? Mostly two things. Treasuries are the government's bonds (Idea 3). Mortgage-backed securities (MBS) are bundles of thousands of home loans: homeowners' monthly payments flow through to whoever owns the bundle. The Fed only buys MBS guaranteed by government-backed agencies (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, Ginnie Mae), so it takes no risk that homeowners default.

Why buy at all? For two different reasons, which is why the size of purchases varies so much. Small, routine purchases of short-dated bills keep enough reserves in the system for payments to run smoothly. Huge purchases of long-dated bonds, QE, aim to push down long-term interest rates when the overnight rate is already at zero (Chapter 8).

What happens next depends on who ultimately sold the bond, which you saw in the lab:

  • A bank sells: the bank swaps a bond for reserves. Reserves rise; the public's money (deposits) does not change.
  • A pension fund, insurer or hedge fund sells (via a dealer): the seller receives a bank deposit and its bank receives reserves. Both reserves and deposits rise. This is how QE added to the money people hold.

Notice what the bank does not get to do in either case: hand the new reserves to the public. As Chapter 2 showed, reserves stay on the Fed's books. QE raises the public's money only through the second route, when non-banks sell.

Does the Fed make a profit?

Usually, yes. The Fed earns interest on the bonds it holds and pays interest on reserves and reverse repos. It works like a bank: profit is the gap between what its assets earn and what its liabilities cost. It sends its profits to the Treasury: tens of billions of dollars a year through the 2010s, close to $100 billion in good years.

Then it flipped. Much of the portfolio was bought in 2020 and 2021 at yields around 1.5%. When the Fed raised rates to fight inflation in 2022, it had to pay over 4% on the reserves that funded those bonds. Earn 1.5%, pay 4%: a loss on every dollar, roughly $100 billion a year at the worst. A normal company would need fresh capital; the Fed doesn't, because it can always pay in reserves it creates. It records the losses as a deferred asset, an IOU to itself that future profits pay down before payments to the Treasury resume.

Fed buys $100 of bonds  →  Fed assets +100 (bonds)  =  Fed liabilities +100 (reserves)
Remember
  • "Printing money" really means the Fed creating reserves to pay for bonds.
  • From a bank: only reserves rise. From a non-bank: reserves and deposits rise.
  • The Fed earns on its bonds and pays on reserves. When rates jump, that gap can turn into losses, which don't stop it working.

Chapter 5

The Fed's toolkit

In plain English: the Fed can't order banks to charge a certain rate. Instead, it sets a floor (what it pays on cash parked with it) and a ceiling (what it charges to lend), and market rates settle in between.

The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meets eight times a year and sets a target range for the federal funds rate, for example 3.75–4.00%. "Federal funds" is just the market name for reserves. Banks that end the day with more reserves than they want lend them overnight to banks that are short, and the rate on those loans is the federal funds rate. It's the shortest, safest rate in the economy, the base that other rates are built on (Idea 5).

Since 2008 the Fed has steered that rate not by rationing reserves but with administered rates: rates the Fed itself pays or charges, which form a corridor. When the news says "the Fed raised rates by a quarter point", what actually happens is that the Fed raises the target range and every administered rate by 0.25 points the next day, and market rates follow within hours.

Why a floor works: a worked example

Suppose the Fed pays 3.90% on reserves (IORB) and some bank offers to lend reserves to other banks at 3.70%. Any bank could borrow from it at 3.70%, leave the money at the Fed earning 3.90%, and pocket 0.20% risk-free. Banks pile in to borrow, which bids the market rate back up. That kind of risk-free trade is called arbitrage, and it's what holds market rates near the Fed's rates. The ceiling works the same way in reverse: nobody pays 6% in the market if the Fed will lend at 4%.

Remember
  • The FOMC sets a target range for the overnight rate banks charge each other.
  • It holds rates in range with a floor (IORB, ON RRP) and a ceiling (SRF, discount window), enforced by arbitrage.
  • A "rate hike" means all those administered rates move up together.

Chapter 6

Scarce, ample and abundant reserves

In plain English: when reserves are hard to come by, banks pay up to borrow them and rates jump. When there are plenty, rates sink to the Fed's floor and stay put. Today the Fed keeps "plenty, but not wastefully many".

Why banks want reserves at all

Banks need reserves to make payments to other banks (Chapter 2), and every day trillions of dollars of payments flow between them. Since 2008, regulators also require big banks to hold a buffer of very safe, instantly available assets, and reserves are the safest of all. Each bank has a level below which it gets nervous. Add those up across the system and you get the demand for reserves.

An analogy: parking in a city

Picture a city where the council pays you a small fee for leaving a parking space empty (that's IORB, the floor). When spaces are scarce, drivers bid fiercely for the last ones and the price shoots up: a few spaces more or less makes a big difference. When spaces are abundant, nobody pays more than the council's fee, and adding another thousand spaces changes nothing. The price is flat. The chart below has exactly this shape: the horizontal axis is the quantity of reserves, the vertical axis is the overnight rate.

From scarce to ample

Before 2008, reserves were scarce: about $10 billion in the whole system. The Fed set rates by adding or draining a few billion each day, moving along the steep part of the curve. QE flooded the system with trillions, and the curve went flat; the old method couldn't work anymore, because nobody could drain trillions overnight. So the Fed adopted an ample reserves framework (formalised in January 2019): it keeps reserves on the flat part and moves rates by changing IORB and the ON RRP rate.

"Ample" means just to the right of the bend: enough that rates are calm, but not so many that the Fed's balance sheet is bigger than it needs to be. The hard part is that nobody knows exactly where the bend is, and it moves as banks' needs change.

Try these: (1) drag reserves left until the marker climbs off the floor; that's September 2019 in miniature. (2) Untick the Standing Repo Facility and drag left again: without a ceiling, rates can spike far above the target range. (3) Move the target range slider and see that the whole picture shifts up or down together, which is what a rate hike does.

Chapter 7

Repo and reverse repo

In plain English: a repo is a very short loan backed by a bond, like a pawn shop for Treasuries. When the Fed lends this way, reserves go up. When money funds lend to the Fed this way (reverse repo), reserves go down.

A repurchase agreement (repo) is a secured overnight loan dressed as two trades: I sell you a Treasury bond today and agree to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price. The price difference is the interest. If I fail to pay, you keep the bond. The US repo market turns over several trillion dollars every day.

The pawn shop version. You hand a pawnbroker your watch and walk out with $100 cash. Tomorrow you bring back $101 and get the watch back. If you don't come back, the pawnbroker sells the watch. A repo is the same thing between financial institutions, with a Treasury bond as the watch. Because the lender holds the bond, the loan is very safe, so repo rates are close to the Fed's rates.

Why it exists. On one side are dealers and hedge funds that own trillions of dollars of bonds bought largely with borrowed money; they need cash every night to keep holding them. On the other side are money market funds with trillions of dollars of savers' cash that needs a safe home overnight. Repo connects them.

The haircut. The lender usually asks for a bit more collateral than the cash it lends: $102 of bonds for a $100 loan. That 2% is the haircut, a cushion in case the bond's price falls before it can be sold.

Every repo has two sides. The cash borrower does a repo; the cash lender does a reverse repo. Same trade, two names, depending on where you stand. The Fed's names follow its own side:

  • Fed repo (Standing Repo Facility, emergency repo operations): the Fed lends cash against collateral. New reserves are created, so reserves go up.
  • Fed reverse repo (ON RRP facility): money market funds lend cash to the Fed overnight. Reserves go down; the cash sits at the Fed, out of the banking system.

The average rate on overnight Treasury repo across the market is published every morning as SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate). It replaced LIBOR as the benchmark for trillions of dollars of loans and derivatives.

September 2019: when repo broke

This episode ties together everything so far: the demand curve (Chapter 6), the TGA draining reserves (Chapter 3) and the need for a ceiling (Chapter 5).

Reserves had fallen from $2.8 trillion in 2014 to about $1.4 trillion, first as currency and the Treasury's cash pile grew, then through QT. On 16 September 2019, corporate tax payments flowed into the TGA (draining reserves further) on the same day a large batch of Treasury auctions settled. Banks were unwilling to lend their remaining reserves, and on 17 September the secured overnight rate SOFR hit 5.25%, more than three points above the top of the Fed's range. The Fed ran its first repo operations in a decade the same morning and in October began buying Treasury bills "to maintain ample reserves". The lesson led to the Standing Repo Facility in 2021.

Why did it matter beyond a few traders? Because repo is how the bond market finances itself. If overnight funding can suddenly cost 5% instead of 2%, dealers can't afford to hold bonds, the Treasury market gets less liquid, and the Fed's control over rates, its main tool, slips. The reverse repo balloon

By 2021 the opposite problem appeared: far more cash than safe places to put it. COVID QE and stimulus had swollen deposits, savers moved cash into money market funds, and those funds couldn't find enough Treasury bills to buy. So they parked it at the ON RRP facility, which paid a safe rate straight from the Fed. Reverse repos at the Fed peaked near $2.7 trillion in April 2023.

That pool then acted as a shock absorber for QT. Think of the Fed's liabilities as connected tanks of water: reserves, reverse repos and the TGA. When QT drains the total, it has to come out of one of them. From 2023 the Treasury issued a flood of bills; money funds pulled cash out of ON RRP to buy them, which emptied the reverse repo tank instead of the reserve tank. So reserves barely fell until the pool was nearly empty in 2025.

How to read the chart. Follow the three lines together. When the shaded reverse repo area grows, reserves tend to dip; when it shrinks, reserves hold up. The TGA line jumps around with tax days and debt-ceiling fights, and reserves move the opposite way.

Chapter 8

QE versus QT

In plain English: when the Fed has already cut rates to zero and the economy still needs help, it buys long-term bonds in bulk to push long-term rates down too (QE). Later, it lets those bonds expire without replacing them (QT).

Quantitative easing is the Fed buying long-dated bonds in bulk, with newly created reserves, once its policy rate is already near zero. Quantitative tightening is the reverse: shrinking the portfolio. The Fed has never sold Treasuries to do QT; it simply stops reinvesting the bonds that mature, up to a monthly cap.

Why QE was invented

In late 2008 the Fed cut the overnight rate to zero, and the economy was still collapsing. It couldn't cut further (people would just hold cash rather than pay to keep money in a bank). But what drives mortgages and business investment is mostly long-term rates, the 10-year and 30-year yields, and those were still well above zero. QE was a way to reach them directly.

How buying bonds lowers long-term rates

Recall Idea 3: when lots of buyers want a bond, its price rises and its yield falls. QE makes the Fed a giant, price-insensitive buyer. It works through three channels:

  1. Less risk left for investors. Holding a 10-year bond is risky because its price swings with rates (the bond chart in Chapter 1). Investors demand extra yield for that risk, called the term premium. When the Fed takes trillions of long bonds out of the market, investors carry less of that risk in total and accept lower yields.
  2. A signal. A central bank that's buying bonds every month isn't about to raise rates. Markets push expected future rates down, and long yields with them.
  3. Portfolio rebalancing. The pension fund that sold bonds to the Fed now holds a deposit earning almost nothing. It still needs returns, so it buys corporate bonds, mortgages or stocks instead, pushing their prices up and their yields down. Cheaper borrowing for companies and higher asset prices then support spending.

Why QT is done by run-off, not selling

Selling trillions of bonds would hammer bond prices, spike long-term rates and force the Fed to lock in losses. Letting bonds mature is gentle and predictable: when a bond the Fed owns comes due, the Treasury repays it, and the Fed simply doesn't buy a replacement. You saw the mechanics in the lab's "QT: a Fed bond matures" button: Fed bonds, reserves and deposits all fall together.

  • QE lowers long-term yields by soaking up risk, signalling low rates and pushing investors into other assets.

Chapter 9

The Fed in a crisis: March 2020

In plain English: in a panic everyone wants cash at the same time and even safe assets become hard to sell. The Fed's job then is to be the buyer and lender of last resort, and it did that on a scale never seen before.

Central banks have had a crisis rulebook since the 1870s, from the British journalist Walter Bagehot: in a panic, lend freely, against good collateral, at a penalty rate. Solvent institutions shouldn't fail just because everyone wants cash on the same day. March 2020 tested that rule harder than ever.

The dash for cash

As COVID lockdowns spread, everyone wanted dollars at once. Companies drew down their credit lines. Foreign central banks sold Treasuries to defend their currencies. Hedge funds had a popular basis trade: buy Treasury bonds, sell slightly pricier Treasury futures, and pocket the tiny gap, multiplied many times over with borrowed repo money. When prices moved the wrong way, they were forced to sell those bonds fast.

Normally dealers buy whatever is sold and resell it later. But dealers have limited room on their own balance sheets (capital rules again), and they filled up. With more sellers than buyers, even Treasuries, the safest assets in the world, became hard to sell, and ten-year yields rose during a flight to safety, the opposite of what usually happens.

The Fed answered with every tool it had, and invented several new ones under the emergency powers of Section 13(3) of the Federal Reserve Act, which lets it lend to non-banks in "unusual and exigent circumstances" with the Treasury's approval. The Treasury put up equity to absorb any losses. Two more tools appear in the timeline: swap lines, which lend dollars to foreign central banks so they don't have to dump Treasuries to get them, and facilities for commercial paper, the short-term IOUs companies use to pay wages and suppliers.

    The money it created

    QE bought the bonds the Treasury issued to fund stimulus checks and the Paycheck Protection Program. The Treasury's spending moved that cash from the TGA into household deposits (the lab's "Treasury spends" button), and the broad money stock grew faster than at any time on record: about 27% in a year.

    What came next: inflation

    In 2021 people spent the money as the economy reopened, while factories, ports and energy supplies were still disrupted. Too much money chasing too few goods. US inflation rose from under 2% to a peak of 9.1% in June 2022, the highest in four decades. Economists still argue about how much was due to the money surge and how much to supply shocks and the war in Ukraine; both mattered.

    The Fed's response was the fastest hiking cycle since the early 1980s: from 0–0.25% in March 2022 to 5.25–5.50% by July 2023, with QT running at the same time. Notice in the chart that M2 then did something it had not done since the 1930s: it shrank, as people paid down debt and moved cash into money market funds that ended up at the Fed's reverse repo facility.

    Two other interventions are worth knowing. In 2008 the Fed lent directly to banks, dealers and AIG and opened swap lines worth over $580 billion before QE1 began. In March 2023, after Silicon Valley Bank failed, it created the Bank Term Funding Program, lending against bonds at face value rather than their depressed market value, so banks did not have to sell at a loss. You can see both as spikes in the "lending" layer of the balance sheet chart.

    SVB is Idea 3 in real life. The bank had put customers' deposits into long-dated bonds bought when rates were near zero. When rates jumped in 2022, those bonds fell sharply in price (drag the bond slider in Chapter 1 from 1% to 5% and look at the 10-year line). Depositors heard about the losses, rushed to withdraw, and the bank had to sell bonds at a loss to pay them. That's a bank run.

    Remember
    • Crisis rule: lend freely, against good collateral, at a penalty rate.
    • In March 2020 even Treasuries were hard to sell; the Fed bought, lent and swapped dollars on a historic scale.
    • The money it created reached households via government spending, M2 jumped, and inflation followed in 2021–22.

    Chapter 11

    What it means for investors

    In plain English: the interest rate the Fed sets is the starting point for how much any future income is worth today. Higher rates make distant profits worth less, which hits the price of growth stocks and long bonds hardest.

    Why rates move stock prices

    A share is a claim on a company's future profits. But $100 arriving in ten years is worth less than $100 today, because you could put a smaller amount in safe Treasuries now and grow it to $100. How much less depends on the interest rate. This is called discounting, and the result is the present value:

    Present value = future amount ÷ (1 + rate)years  ·  $100 in 10 years at 2% = $82; at 5% = $61

    Investors use the risk-free Treasury yield, plus a premium for risk, as that rate. So when rates rise, every stock's future profits are discounted harder. Far-off profits are hit most, the same way long bonds are hit most. That's why fast-growing tech companies, whose profits lie mostly in the future, fell hardest when the Fed hiked in 2022. Try it:

    A watch list

    Plumbing does not set the value of a business, but it changes the price investors pay for it, especially at the margin. A short watch list:

    • The policy rate and its path. The discount rate in every valuation model starts with the risk-free rate. Forward guidance moves it before the Fed acts.
    • Bank reserves. When reserves approach the scarce zone, funding markets get jumpy (2019) and the Fed tends to stop QT.
    • The TGA. When the Treasury rebuilds its cash (after a debt-ceiling fight, or on tax days), it drains reserves; when it spends down, it adds them.
    • ON RRP. Cash sitting at the Fed is idle. Its drain from 2023 to 2025 quietly offset QT.

    Some analysts compress these into one number, "net liquidity": Fed assets minus the TGA minus reverse repos. The logic: start with everything the Fed has created (its assets), then subtract the parts that are parked out of reach of banks and markets, the Treasury's cash and the money funds' cash at the Fed. What's left is roughly the money sloshing around the financial system. It is a rough heuristic, not a law, but it shows how QE, the Treasury's account and the RRP pool combine. Compare the two lines: in 2023 the Fed's assets were falling (QT), but net liquidity held up because the reverse repo pool was draining.

    Reference

    Glossary and sources

    Arbitrage
    A risk-free profit from a price gap, e.g. borrowing at 3.7% and earning 3.9% at the Fed. Arbitrage closes such gaps quickly.
    Asset / liability / equity
    What you own / what you owe / what's left over. Assets always equal liabilities plus equity.
    Balance sheet
    A list of an institution's assets on one side and liabilities plus equity on the other.
    Basis point (bp)
    One hundredth of a percentage point. 0.25% = 25 bp.
    Bond, coupon, maturity
    A tradeable loan; the fixed interest it pays; the date the principal is repaid.
    Capital requirements
    Rules forcing banks to fund part of their assets with their own equity, so losses hit owners before depositors.
    Collateral
    An asset pledged to secure a loan, which the lender keeps if the borrower doesn't repay.
    Deposits
    Money in bank accounts. A liability of the bank, created mostly when banks lend.
    Discount window
    The Fed's oldest lending facility for banks, at a rate at the top of the target range.
    Dual mandate
    The Fed's two goals set by Congress: maximum employment and stable prices (2% inflation).
    Duration
    How sensitive a bond's price is to interest rates. Longer bonds have more duration.
    EFFR
    Effective federal funds rate: the average rate on overnight unsecured loans of reserves between banks.
    FOMC
    Federal Open Market Committee: the 12 voting officials who set US monetary policy, eight times a year.
    Forward guidance
    The Fed telling markets where it expects rates to go.
    H.4.1
    The Fed's weekly balance sheet release, published every Thursday.
    Haircut
    The extra collateral a lender demands beyond the cash lent, as a safety cushion.
    Inflation
    The rate at which the general level of prices rises, measured over a year.
    IORB
    Interest on reserve balances: the rate the Fed pays banks on reserves.
    M2
    A broad measure of the public's money: cash, checking and savings deposits, small time deposits and retail money funds.
    MBS
    Mortgage-backed securities: bonds made from bundles of home loans.
    Money market fund
    A fund holding only short, safe IOUs; used like a high-yield bank account.
    ON RRP
    Overnight reverse repo facility: money funds and others lend cash to the Fed overnight.
    Present value
    What a future payment is worth today, after discounting at an interest rate.
    Primary dealers
    About two dozen banks and brokers that trade directly with the Fed and bid at Treasury auctions.
    QE / QT
    Quantitative easing / tightening: the Fed growing / shrinking its bond holdings.
    Repo / reverse repo
    A short loan made by selling a bond and agreeing to buy it back. The cash borrower does a repo; the cash lender a reverse repo.
    Reserves
    Banks' deposits at the Fed. Created only by the Fed; used to settle payments between banks.
    SOFR
    Secured Overnight Financing Rate: the average rate on overnight Treasury repo.
    SOMA
    System Open Market Account: the Fed's portfolio of securities.
    SRF
    Standing Repo Facility: the Fed lends cash overnight against Treasuries and agency debt.
    Swap lines
    Agreements to lend dollars to foreign central banks in exchange for their currency.
    Term premium
    The extra yield investors demand for holding a long bond instead of rolling short ones.
    TGA
    Treasury General Account: the government's checking account at the Fed.
    Treasury bills / bonds
    US government IOUs. Bills mature within a year; notes and bonds run up to 30 years.
    Yield
    The annual return a buyer locks in by buying a bond at today's price.
