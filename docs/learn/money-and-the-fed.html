Chapter 1

What is a dollar?

A dollar is not one thing. It is a promise recorded on somebody's balance sheet, and there are three kinds of promise that all trade at exactly one dollar:

  • Physical currency. A $20 note is a liability of the Federal Reserve. Anyone can hold it.
  • Bank reserves. Balances that commercial banks keep in their accounts at the Fed. Only banks (and a few other institutions) can hold them. Banks use them to pay each other.
  • Bank deposits. The number in your checking account is a liability of your bank, not of the Fed. This is the money households and firms actually spend, and it is by far the largest layer.

The layers form a hierarchy. Deposits are promises to pay you reserves-or-cash on demand; reserves and cash are promises of the state's central bank, which can never run out of its own liabilities. Who can create each layer is the subject of this whole lecture.

Chapter 2

How dollars are made: the money lab

The textbook story says banks collect savings and lend them out. The real mechanics run the other way: when a bank makes a loan, it creates a brand-new deposit for the borrower. Loans create deposits; repaying a loan destroys them. The Bank of England put this plainly in its 2014 paper Money creation in the modern economy.

What limits banks is not a stock of savings but profitability, capital requirements, liquidity rules and demand for credit. Since March 2020, US reserve requirements are zero, so the old "money multiplier" no longer describes anything.

The Fed creates the other two layers. It creates reserves whenever it buys an asset or lends, simply by crediting a bank's account. It issues currency when banks order cash: the Bureau of Engraving and Printing prints the notes, and a bank pays for them with reserves. The Treasury does not create money; it taxes and borrows, and keeps its cash in an account at the Fed called the Treasury General Account (TGA).

Chapter 3

How the Fed "buys money"

The phrase "the Fed prints money" is shorthand for something more precise. The Fed's trading desk at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York buys Treasury bonds or mortgage-backed securities from about two dozen primary dealers. It pays by crediting the dealer's bank with reserves that did not exist a second earlier. No paper is printed. The bonds go into the Fed's portfolio, the System Open Market Account (SOMA).

What happens next depends on who ultimately sold the bond, which you saw in the lab:

  • A bank sells: the bank swaps a bond for reserves. Reserves rise; the public's money (deposits) does not change.
  • A pension fund, insurer or hedge fund sells (via a dealer): the seller receives a bank deposit and its bank receives reserves. Both reserves and deposits rise. This is how QE added to the money people hold.

The Fed earns interest on the bonds it holds and pays interest on reserves and reverse repos. It sends its profits to the Treasury: tens of billions of dollars a year through the 2010s, close to $100 billion in good years. When rates rose above the yield on its old low-coupon bonds in late 2022, the Fed began losing money. It does not need capital to keep operating; it records the losses as a deferred asset that future profits pay down before remittances resume.

Fed buys $100 of bonds  →  Fed assets +100 (bonds)  =  Fed liabilities +100 (reserves)
Chapter 4

The Fed's toolkit

The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meets eight times a year and sets a target range for the federal funds rate, the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans of reserves. Since 2008 it has steered that rate not by rationing reserves but with administered rates: rates the Fed itself pays or charges, which form a corridor.

Wednesday values, percent. IOER (interest on excess reserves) was replaced by IORB (interest on reserve balances) in July 2021.

Read the corridor from the top down. Nobody lends reserves below IORB to another bank, because the Fed pays IORB risk-free. Money market funds can't earn IORB, but they can lend to the Fed at the ON RRP rate, so private rates rarely fall below that floor. And no one needs to pay much above the top of the range, because banks can borrow at the discount window or, since 2021, from the Standing Repo Facility. The effective fed funds rate (EFFR) stays inside.

Chapter 5

Scarce, ample and abundant reserves

Before 2008, reserves were scarce: about $10 billion in the whole system. The Fed set rates by adding or draining a few billion each day, moving along the steep part of the demand curve below. QE flooded the system with trillions, and the curve went flat. Now the Fed runs an ample reserves framework (formalised in January 2019): it keeps reserves on the flat part and moves rates by changing IORB and the ON RRP rate.

Chapter 6

Repo and reverse repo

A repurchase agreement (repo) is a secured overnight loan dressed as two trades: I sell you a Treasury bond today and agree to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price. The price difference is the interest. If I fail to pay, you keep the bond. The US repo market turns over several trillion dollars every day; it is how dealers and hedge funds finance their bond holdings.

Every repo has two sides. The cash borrower does a repo; the cash lender does a reverse repo. The Fed's names follow its own side:

  • Fed repo (Standing Repo Facility, emergency repo operations): the Fed lends cash against collateral. Reserves go up.
  • Fed reverse repo (ON RRP facility): money market funds lend cash to the Fed overnight. Reserves go down; the cash sits at the Fed.

September 2019: when repo broke

Reserves had fallen from $2.8 trillion in 2014 to about $1.4 trillion, first as currency and the Treasury's cash pile grew, then through QT. On 16 September 2019, corporate tax payments flowed into the TGA (draining reserves further) on the same day a large batch of Treasury auctions settled. Banks were unwilling to lend their remaining reserves, and on 17 September the secured overnight rate SOFR hit 5.25%, more than three points above the top of the Fed's range. The Fed ran its first repo operations in a decade the same morning and in October began buying Treasury bills "to maintain ample reserves". The lesson led to the Standing Repo Facility in 2021.

The reverse repo balloon

By 2021 the opposite problem appeared: far more cash than safe places to put it. Money funds parked it at the ON RRP facility, and reverse repos at the Fed peaked near $2.7 trillion in April 2023. That pool then acted as a shock absorber for QT: as the Fed shrank its balance sheet and the Treasury issued a flood of bills, money funds pulled cash out of ON RRP to buy them, so reserves barely fell until the pool was nearly empty in 2025.

Chapter 7

QE versus QT

Quantitative easing is the Fed buying long-dated bonds in bulk, with newly created reserves, once its policy rate is already near zero. Quantitative tightening is the reverse: shrinking the portfolio. The Fed has never sold Treasuries to do QT; it simply stops reinvesting the bonds that mature, up to a monthly cap.

Chapter 8

The Fed in a crisis: March 2020

In March 2020 even the safest market in the world broke. Foreign central banks, hedge funds unwinding leveraged "basis trades", and companies drawing credit lines all tried to sell Treasuries for cash at once, and dealers ran out of balance sheet to absorb them. Ten-year yields rose during a flight to safety. The Fed answered with every tool it had, and invented several new ones under the emergency powers of Section 13(3) of the Federal Reserve Act, with the Treasury putting up equity to absorb losses.

    The money it created

    QE bought the bonds the Treasury issued to fund stimulus checks and the Paycheck Protection Program. The Treasury's spending moved that cash from the TGA into household deposits, and the broad money stock grew faster than at any time on record.

    Two other interventions are worth knowing. In 2008 the Fed lent directly to banks, dealers and AIG and opened swap lines worth over $580 billion before QE1 began. In March 2023, after Silicon Valley Bank failed, it created the Bank Term Funding Program, lending against bonds at face value rather than their depressed market value, so banks did not have to sell at a loss.     What it means for investors

    Plumbing does not set the value of a business, but it changes the price investors pay for it, especially at the margin. A short watch list:

    • The policy rate and its path. The discount rate in every valuation model starts with the risk-free rate. Forward guidance moves it before the Fed acts.
    • Bank reserves. When reserves approach the scarce zone, funding markets get jumpy (2019) and the Fed tends to stop QT.
    • The TGA. When the Treasury rebuilds its cash (after a debt-ceiling fight, or on tax days), it drains reserves; when it spends down, it adds them.
    • ON RRP. Cash sitting at the Fed is idle. Its drain from 2023 to 2025 quietly offset QT.

    Some analysts compress these into one number, "net liquidity": Fed assets minus the TGA minus reverse repos. It is a rough heuristic, not a law, but it shows how QE, the Treasury's account and the RRP pool combine.

    Reserves
    Banks' deposits at the Fed. Created only by the Fed.
    IORB
    Interest on reserve balances: the rate the Fed pays banks on reserves.

    Overnight reverse repo facility: money funds and others lend cash to the Fed overnight.

    Standing Repo Facility: the Fed lends cash overnight against Treasuries and agency debt.

    Secured Overnight Financing Rate: the average rate on overnight Treasury repo.

    Effective federal funds rate: the average rate on overnight unsecured loans of reserves between banks.

    Treasury General Account: the government's checking account at the Fed.

    System Open Market Account: the Fed's portfolio of securities.
