Does it work? Backtest since 2000

Each day since 2000 the gauge was computed with only the data available that day. That reading is then compared with the bond's return over the following six months, in excess of 3-month T-bills. Returns come from a constant-maturity bond priced off the daily Treasury yield (2-, 5- and 20-year), because the ETFs don't have that much history. Rank IC is the Spearman correlation between the gauge and the forward return: 0 means no information, and anything above 0.05 is useful for a macro signal.

How the gauges are built

Each gauge averages six or seven indicators. Every indicator is scored from −1 (conditions favour selling that part of the curve) to +1 (they favour buying it), and the gauge is the average × 100. Zones: Strong sell below −50, Sell −50 to −20, Hold −20 to +20, Buy +20 to +50, Strong buy above +50.

Two kinds of scoring are used. Indicators with a natural scale (the Sahm rule, inflation against the 2% target, 3-month changes) use fixed linear ranges. Valuation indicators that drift over decades (real yields, the term premium, the curve slope) use a rolling 10-year z-score, so "cheap" means cheap against the last decade, not against 1981.

The three buckets react to different things:

  • Short-term (0–3y) trades the Fed. Front-end yields are roughly the expected average policy rate, so the gauge reads the policy stance (real fed funds rate), its direction, the labour market that drives it (Sahm rule, jobless claims), core inflation, and 2-year yield momentum.
  • Mid-term (3–10y) trades the business cycle. Excess bond returns are countercyclical: they are high in slowdowns and low in expansions (Ludvigson & Ng, 2009). The gauge combines economic activity (CFNAI), the labour market, inflation and its expectations, the 5-year real yield as valuation, and trend.
  • Long-term (10y+) trades valuation and risk. It uses the term premium (Kim–Wright), the 10-year real yield (the bond "value" factor of Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, 2013), the curve slope (Campbell & Shiller, 1991; Fama & Bliss, 1987), the yield against nominal GDP growth, inflation-expectation momentum, trend (time-series momentum, Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen, 2012) and flight to quality (credit spreads).

Data: FRED (St. Louis Fed) for every macro series and Yahoo Finance for ETF prices, refreshed every weekday. Monthly and quarterly releases only enter the model after their usual publication lag (unemployment +5 weeks, core PCE +2 months, GDP +4 months), so the history and the backtest never use data before it was published. FRED serves the latest revision of each series, though, so revised data can still flatter the backtest a little.

The scales and thresholds were set from economic reasoning before looking at returns, not optimised on them. That keeps the backtest honest, but it is still one sample of 25 years with few full rate cycles. Treat the gauges as a structured read of conditions, not as a trading system.
